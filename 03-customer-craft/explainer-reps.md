# Explainer reps

You will spend a lot of this job explaining why you chose X over Y to people who have never shipped software, and some of them will have confident opinions from ChatGPT. Jargon doesn't win that room. A plain explanation, with one trade-off and a clear ask, does.

The drill is volume. Record yourself explaining one concept a few times a week, in 2 minutes, to an imagined non-technical listener: a clinic CEO, a CFO, a head of front desk. Nobody has to see these recordings. Their first job is to show you where your own understanding is thin. If you can't explain chunk overlap without saying "embedding", you don't understand it well enough yet.

## How to do one rep

1. Pick a concept from the list below and a listener (write it down: "Kwame, Lakeside CEO").
2. Record 2 minutes, no notes, no script. Use Loom, OBS or your phone.
3. Watch it back once and score it against the rubric below.
4. If it scored under 8, re-record it the same day. Log both attempts.
5. Every fourth rep, play one for a non-technical friend and ask them to repeat it back. What they get wrong is your score.

## Rubric (2 points each, 10 total)

| | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No jargon** | 3+ unexplained terms | 1–2 | None, or each one explained the moment you used it |
| **One analogy** | None | An analogy that needed its own explanation | One analogy from the listener's world (their clinic, their ledger, their market stall) |
| **One trade-off** | No trade-off at all, so it sounds like magic | Mentioned but vague | Stated what you give up, in their terms: money, time, risk |
| **Answer first** | Built up to the point | The point comes in the middle | The point lands in the first 15 seconds (Minto) |
| **Clear ask or decision** | Just stopped | "Any questions?" | Ends on what you need from them, or the decision they now have to make |

Watch for filler words, apologies ("this is a bit technical, but…") and going over time. Each of those costs a point under *No jargon* or *Answer first*.

## Concepts (do all 15, in any order)

Each one is something a customer has actually asked, or will.

| # | Concept | Listener |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Why the AI sometimes gets invoices wrong, and what we do about it | CFO |
| 2 | What an eval is, and why I won't go live without one | CEO |
| 3 | Why the documents get cut into overlapping chunks before the AI searches them | Head of compliance |
| 4 | What reranking does, and why it's worth the extra half second | Product manager |
| 5 | RAG vs. "just give the AI all our documents" | CEO who read about million-token context windows |
| 6 | Why some cases go to a human, and how we picked the cut-off | Head of front desk |
| 7 | Why it can't be live by next Friday (it can be in 6 weeks, and here's what we ship each week) | CEO with ChatGPT open |
| 8 | Why we're not using five agents talking to each other | CTO who saw a vendor demo |
| 9 | Prompt injection: how a document can give the AI orders, and what stops it | IT lead |
| 10 | Why cost per completed conversation matters more than the price per token | CFO |
| 11 | Why the Twi answers are worse than the English ones, and what we're doing about it | Clinic CEO |
| 12 | Why the AI gives a slightly different answer each time, and when that's fine | Operations manager |
| 13 | Why a cheaper model for easy cases and a stronger one for hard cases | CFO |
| 14 | What happens to patient data the AI sees: where it goes, who sees it, when it's deleted | Medical director |
| 15 | Why the pilot needs 200 of your real invoices before we can promise a number | Finance director |

Then add your own: every time a stakeholder call or inject card leaves you fumbling for words, that topic becomes a rep.
